Behind the blend

How this one was made

Before there was tea in a bag, there was a book. We wrote down a method for balancing herbs and put it in a form a machine could read. Buckshot & Gunpowder was built with that method, then weighed and mixed by hand in Montana.

The book came first

Every tradition of plant medicine, in every language, starts from the same observation. Illness is imbalance. What those traditions never shared was a common structure. Greek physicians read the body in pairs of opposed qualities, hot against cold, moist against dry. Chinese philosophy read it as two forces that each carry the seed of the other. Aristotle put virtue at the mean between excess and deficiency. Modern biology calls the same pattern homeostasis, and adds that dose is what separates a small correction from a poison.

Those are four accounts of one idea, and nobody had put them into a single working framework that a person could actually build a formula with. So we wrote one. It's called the Apothecary's Code, and it lays out what we call the Wheel: four energetic quadrants, four directions of movement, four organ axes, four flavor influences, and a ratio doctrine that gives a blend its structure. It's a method for building a formula. It isn't a list of herbs and it makes no claim about disease.

Then we taught it to the machine

The Code was written to be read either by a person or by a machine, which is unusual for an herbal text and was the whole point of writing it. Underneath it sits a shelf of herbals: Culpeper's Complete Herbal from 1653, Fernie's Herbal Simples, King's American Dispensatory, Millspaugh's American Medicinal Plants, and a set of modern clinical and regulatory references to keep the old books honest.

Working from the Code, the conversation was about proportion. Which roots dominate and which get buried. Where the bitterness lands. What the rooibos does to the gunpowder green. The blend came out in parts rather than percentages: gunpowder green at four parts, rooibos and fenugreek at two each, four herbs at one part, five at three quarters, and orange peel at a quarter. Then the herbs were weighed out, mixed, and brewed. That's the part no software does. It was good, so it became the recipe.
