Gunpowder Green Tea

A leaf rolled into pellets, a name that arrived four hundred years late, and the most repeated origin story in tea, which nobody has ever been able to source.

From Gunpowder Pearls to Moroccan Mint
Season 1 · Episode 1

Open a tin of gunpowder green tea and it does not look like tea. It looks like shot. Small, hard, grey-green pellets that rattle when you pour them, closer to something you would load than something you would drink.

That is the whole trick, and it is a shipping decision rather than a flavour one. Each pellet is a leaf rolled tight. Rolled that way it takes up less room, holds less air, and survives a long sea voyage that would leave a loose leaf as stale hay. What it costs in delicacy it repays in durability. Gunpowder is not the fine porcelain of the tea world. It is the tea that travelled.

What the rolling does

Before rolling, the leaf goes through what Chinese processing calls kill-green. Heat is applied in a pan or a drum, hot enough and fast enough to shut down the enzymes that would otherwise turn a green leaf brown. It is the same reason a cut apple goes tan on the counter and why lemon juice stops it.

Kill-green is where gunpowder gets its character. Pan-firing leaves a toasted, faintly smoky edge that no steamed Japanese green has. Then the leaf is rolled while still pliable, and dried hard.

The cup that comes out is brisk, astringent, and toasty. It is a bold tea and an assertive one, which matters when you are asking it to carry twelve other plants.

The name arrived late

The Chinese name is 珠茶, zhū chá, which means pearl tea. That is the older name and the more accurate one. The pellets are pearls, not powder.

Gunpowder is what English-speaking traders called it, and the usual explanations are that the pellets look like cannon powder, or that the leaf seems to detonate as it unrolls in hot water. Both are reasonable. Neither is documented, and they have the flavour of stories invented after the fact to explain a name that was already stuck.

What is certain is that the name is a translation of nothing. Nobody in the tea's own language ever called it gunpowder.

The story everybody tells

Here is the version you will find on most tea sites, ours included until we checked.

During the Crimean War, British merchants found their Baltic and Russian markets blockaded and were left holding warehouses of Chinese green tea they could not sell. In desperation they offloaded it at Tangier and Mogador. Morocco, which had been drinking wormwood and sage, found that this smoky, robust leaf took mint and sugar better than anything local, and a national drink was born out of a trade accident.

It is a wonderful story. It is repeated everywhere. And as far as anyone can tell, it is not sourced.

The historian Graham Cornwell, in Sweetening the Pot: A History of Tea and Sugar in Morocco, 1850 to 1960, went looking for the evidence behind it and found that none of the authors advancing the Crimean War thesis offer any evidence or cite any source at all. It is a claim that has been passed hand to hand until repetition made it feel established.

What the trade record does show runs the other way round. Britain needed grain to feed an army. Moroccan grain exports hit their highest level in decades in 1856. If the war put tea in Moroccan glasses, the likelier route is that Morocco earned British money selling wheat and then spent it, rather than Britain dumping stock it could not move.

We are keeping the story on this page because it is worth knowing how much of what everyone knows about tea is like this. We are not going to tell it as fact.

What is not in doubt

The trade itself is documented and the numbers are startling.

Morocco is the world's largest importer of green tea, taking something on the order of 82,000 tonnes a year, and almost all of it is gunpowder. Roughly 46 percent of global exports of the gunpowder grade end up there, along with about a quarter of all Chinese green tea exports. Chinese producers run grades made specifically for that market, which is why a trade code like 3505 means something precise to a buyer in Casablanca and nothing at all to a shopper in Denver.

A country that grows no tea became the centre of gravity for an entire category. However it started, that part is real.

Weigh it, do not spoon it

The rolling that makes gunpowder durable also makes it deceptive in the cup.

A pellet is dense. It has been compressed, and it has far less air in it than a fluffy white tea or an open-leaf green. So a spoonful of gunpowder is a great deal more tea than a spoonful of almost anything else, and measuring by volume is how people end up with a cup that is harsh and bitter and conclude they do not like it.

Use a scale. The pellet is a slow-release object and it wants time to open. Given that, it gives up a strong first cup and a second that is sweeter and smoother, which most green teas will not do at all.

Bitter, sweet, gentle

In the Maghreb the pouring is a ritual, and there is a proverb attached to it:

The first glass is as bitter as life, the second is as sweet as love, and the third is as gentle as death.

Read it as poetry and it is lovely. Read it as a description of an extraction curve and it is also accurate. The first pour carries the most caffeine and the most tannin, so it bites. By the second the leaf has opened and sugar has gone in, so it rounds out. By the third the leaf is nearly spent and what is left is mild.

The proverb is doing what a good tasting note does. It is just older, and better written.

Why it leads this blend

Gunpowder is a quarter of Buckshot & Gunpowder by weight, the largest single share of the thirteen.

It is there because it is strong enough to hold a floor under a dozen other plants without disappearing, and clean enough to let the ginger and turmeric and peppercorn come through on top of it. A gentler tea would be buried. A more perfumed one would argue with the roots.
